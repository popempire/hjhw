## Lagan Dream

 >    How cheerfully he seems to grin
 >    How neatly spreads his claws
 >    And welcomes little fishes in
 >    With gently smiling jaws

This is where the elephant enters the room.
These lines are from the well-known children's book *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*
by Lewis Carroll. Like millions of children, I was introduced to this work through
Disney's animated adaptation, *Alice in Wonderland*. As entertaining and memorable
as the movie was, I found the books even more captivating when we read them as a
family in my childhood (the Disney film is an amalgamation of *Wonderland* and
*Through the Looking-Glass*, another children's novel by Carroll featuring the 
same protagonist.)

I was drawn to the whimsy, the sense of mystery and magic with
a tinge of menace, reminiscent of the old-time fairytales. I was drawn to the
psychedelia. Most especially, I was drawn to the curious poems and song lyrics
interspersed through the text. "Jabberwocky," probably the most famous of these, 
was a canonical poem for me in my formative years. But all of the poetry in these
volumes held interest for me. It rolled with a self-assured nursery rhyme rhythm,
and dealt even-handedly in sense and nonsense. I found it inspiring, and I would
often flip through the pages of my copy of the books (a dual volume) to scan for 
the fragments of verse found within.

I knew a few historical details concerning the authorship and inspiration of these
books. I knew that Carroll was a professor of mathematics by trade, and that the 
character Alice was based on a real girl named Alice who was the daughter of a
family with whom Carroll was friendly. The books were apparently written originally 
for her amusement before finding a wider audience.

What I did not know, and wish I had realized sooner, was that Lewis Carroll was
a pedophile.

### How did I wind up using the poetry of a pedophile in my song?

I have long loved the song "My Lagan Love". For years I had been conceiving of a
way to marry this traditional Irish air with a dark trip-hop style production.
I think that, broadly speaking, I achieved the musical vision that I had for this
song, with the inestimable help of my collaborators: Jake Langknecht first of all,
along with Isaac Karns (mixing), Keegan Hanrahan (saxophone), and Kelsey Cork
(who sang and played on the first demo I made of this song). I'm immensely proud
of the arrangement and production we executed on this track.

That said, it was only after the song was done, recorded and released, that I 
learned about Carroll's true nature. My immediate reaction was to recoil in shame,
and I felt a regret for including his words in my recording. The four lines in
my recording that come from him are not material to the song and could have been
replaced by any number of lines that fit the rhythm (a very common meter for 
English poetry). It'd truly be a pity if Carroll's legacy diminished the joy that
anyone might feel from this song.

From where I stand now, however, it seems like the only good path forward is to
drag everything out into the light. And perhaps, by doing this unpleasant work,
I can actually do something psychologically useful that otherwise never would
have been done.

The mistake (i.e. the decision to include Carroll's lines in my lyrics)
was made innocently enough, though I could reproach myself for not more thoroughly
vetting my sources. I could also reproach the culture I've been born into, for
presenting Carroll's work as celebrated classics of children's literature —
this is a discussion I will take up later. But for now, I will simply relate the
sequence of events in my songwriting process as they occurred, without any
sensationalism.

As much as I wanted to record a new version of "My Lagan Love," I felt that the
traditional lyrics, while brilliant in certain places, stood in need of an update
in others. Here's what I was working with, the original English translation of an
air presumably composed in Irish Gaelic (skipping ahead to the relevant verse):

 >    And often when the beetle's horn
 >    Hath lulled the eve to sleep
 >    I steal unto her sheiling lorn
 >    And through the door in-peep
 >    There on the cricket's singing-stone
 >    She spares the bogwood fire
 >    And hums in sad, sweet undertone
 >    The song of heart's desire

I love the imagery, rhythm, and soundscape throughout these lines. And for the last
four lines I had no notes — they're perfect, and I left them as-is in my version.
But the first four lines of this verse made me uncomfortable to sing. It's
patently voyeuristic, and feels creepy, even if the singer is truly in love with
the object of his attention.

Not to worry, I figured. As I've already mentioned, the metrical rhythm here is
very common in English song and poetry. It's basically a common nursery rhyme
meter, also popular for hymns ("Amazing Grace," for example). I knew I could draw
from an almost unlimited supply of existing verses to replace these four lines.
I was interested in doing it this way, because I felt that a medley of existing
lyrics would help build the dreamlike atmosphere I was trying to generate.

So, I took a page from my childhood habit and started leafing through my copy of
*Alice's Adventures in Wonderland* and *Through the Looking-Glass*. I came upon
"The Crocodile."

This poem is a play on a classic English morality poem from 1715 by Isaac Watts,
"How Doth the Little Busy Bee," originally titled, "Against Idleness and Mischief."

 >    How doth the little busy bee
      Improve each shining hour,
      And gather honey all the day
      From every opening flower!
      
      How skilfully she builds her cell!
      How neat she spreads the wax!
      And labors hard to store it well
      With the sweet food she makes.
      
      In works of labor or of skill,
      I would be busy too;
      For Satan finds some mischief still
      For idle hands to do.
      
      In books, or work, or healthful play,
      Let my first years be passed,
      That I may give for every day
      Some good account at last.
